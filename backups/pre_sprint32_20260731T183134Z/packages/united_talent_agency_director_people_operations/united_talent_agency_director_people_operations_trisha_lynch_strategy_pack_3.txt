# Trisha Lynch | Strategy Pack

## United Talent Agency | Director, People Operations

### Role Opportunity Brief

Based on the posting, the Director, People Operations role is responsible for turning People strategy into programs, systems, reporting, and daily operating practices that can scale with United Talent Agency. The work brings together cross-functional program leadership, continuous improvement, clear executive updates, team leadership, and practical implementation. Its success will depend on whether managers and teams understand the programs, trust the operating model, and can use it without unnecessary friction.

The posting also includes requirements that should be treated distinctly. Cross-functional alignment, workflow design, stakeholder communication, implementation, and leading complex teams have strong parallels in Trisha's professional operations background. Direct People Operations tenure, employee-lifecycle ownership, People policy, organizational design, and People data expertise are not established by her evidence and should remain visible gaps. A thoughtful first move would be to listen to the People team and its partners, map how programs and decisions currently move, and identify where clearer ownership, communication, or standards would improve both business outcomes and the day-to-day experience of the work.

### Why Trisha

Trisha's fit is grounded in transferable operations leadership, not a claim of traditional HR experience. At OMG23 / Omnicom Media Group, she led cross-functional teams of 60+ across creative, marketing, media, analytics, technology, and operations. The work required listening across functions, clarifying responsibilities, improving communication, and helping teams use shared workflows and standards under demanding conditions.

She also introduced scalable governance practices and execution standards across several functions. The meaningful result was not more process. It was clearer ownership, more dependable handoffs, and a common understanding of what good execution required. That maps well to the parts of People Operations centered on program implementation, change adoption, team effectiveness, and operational consistency. She would be transparent about the areas where her background is adjacent, including employee-lifecycle programs, People policy, organizational design, and People analytics. Her value is a practical, observant approach to building systems around how people actually work in a complex, fast-moving environment.

### 30/60/90-Day Plan

#### First 30 Days: Listen, Map, and Understand

- Meet People leaders, People Operations specialists, managers, and key partner teams to understand priorities and working expectations.
- Review active People programs, reporting rhythms, service expectations, workflows, and the current paths for ownership and escalation.
- Map how business priorities become People programs, team actions, decisions, and executive updates.
- Listen for recurring friction in communication, handoffs, adoption, and day-to-day use without assuming every issue needs a new process.
- Confirm where direct HR expertise is essential and where transferable operating support can add value.

#### Days 31-60: Prioritize, Align, and Improve

- Separate isolated concerns from recurring ownership, communication, workflow, or implementation problems.
- Align stakeholders on a short list of practical improvements with clear owners, audiences, and measures.
- Test lightweight changes to documentation, handoffs, reporting, or team routines with the people closest to the work.
- Improve decision-ready updates for People leadership while keeping reporting useful to operating teams.
- Define how feedback from managers and teams will shape changes before they scale.

#### Days 61-90: Operationalize, Learn, and Scale

- Turn effective early improvements into repeatable practices teams can understand and sustain.
- Establish practical governance for ownership, escalation, service expectations, and cross-functional follow-through.
- Improve visibility into progress and friction without creating unnecessary administrative burden.
- Agree on measures that connect business outcomes with adoption and team experience.
- Prepare a longer-term roadmap that distinguishes operating improvements from work requiring specialized HR ownership.

### Strategic POV Note

#### How I Think About This Role

People Operations is most useful when business priorities and the lived experience of work are considered together. A program can be sound on paper and still fail if ownership is unclear, managers do not understand the decision, or teams cannot use the process in their daily work. The operating question is therefore not only whether a system is efficient. It is whether people understand it, trust it, and know what to do next.

My perspective comes from operations rather than a traditional HR organization. I have listened across functions with different pressures and built workflows, responsibilities, standards, and communication practices around complex work. That background does not replace expertise in employee relations, People policy, HR systems, or organizational design. It does offer a grounded way to translate leadership priorities into practices teams can adopt without adding process for its own sake.

I would begin by learning how work happens now. Where do managers lose context? Which handoffs create repeated confusion? What are teams doing outside the formal process to keep work moving? Those observations make it possible to distinguish a documentation problem from an ownership problem, a reporting problem from a decision problem, and a one-time issue from a pattern worth addressing.

The goal is practical consistency with room for judgment. Clear expectations, useful feedback loops, and proportionate governance can make People programs easier to operate while respecting the people who use them. I would bring that balance to the role: support the business need, listen closely to the human context, and build enough structure for teams to move forward with confidence.

### Interview Talking Points

- **Transparent transferable fit:** My background is in operations rather than traditional HR, and I can be specific about where team leadership, change adoption, and workflow implementation transfer.
- **Listening for operational friction:** I start by understanding how teams actually work, where context disappears, and which points of friction recur.
- **Helping different functions work together:** I aligned teams with different priorities and working styles around shared responsibilities, decision paths, and dependable handoffs.
- **Ownership and communication clarity:** I introduced workflows, responsibilities, standards, and communication practices that made handoffs more dependable.
- **Adoption without bureaucracy:** I focus on the lightest useful structure and whether people understand, trust, and can sustain a change.
- **Knowing the evidence boundary:** I would not claim direct ownership of employee relations, People policy, HRIS, organizational design, or People analytics.

### Smart Questions to Ask

- Where do managers and teams experience the most recurring friction in current People programs or processes?
- Which responsibilities require deep HR subject-matter ownership, and where would strong cross-functional operating leadership add the most value?
- How does feedback from managers, employees, and People Operations specialists shape program improvements today?
- Which reporting rhythms help People leadership make decisions, and where is context still difficult to see?
- What changes are teams being asked to adopt, and what has made adoption easier or harder?
- How does the team balance consistency with the needs of different functions and employee groups?
- By the end of 90 days, what evidence would show that this leader is improving both execution and the experience of the work?
